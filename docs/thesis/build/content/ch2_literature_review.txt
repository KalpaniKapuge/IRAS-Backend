#C CHAPTER 02 - LITERATURE REVIEW

# 2.1 Chapter Overview

This chapter discusses the literature reviewed for the Intelligent Recruitment Automation System. It reviews prior research on using AI for recruitment, resume assessment and matching candidates with job descriptions, automated job description generation, candidate ranking, skill-gap analysis, feedback generation, user profiling, CV generation, interview scheduling, notifications, knowledge base support, and AI-assisted recruitment chatbots.

The chapter aims to explain the relevance of previous research in recruitment automation from several perspectives and to identify how existing work has covered different aspects of the problem. It offers an objective critique of the contribution and shortcomings of each study rather than a mere summary, which helps determine the research gap for a unified recruitment automation system integrating candidate, employer, and admin workflows with AI-assisted and automated recruitment features.

# 2.3 Domain Overview

Recruitment is the process of attracting, screening, and selecting appropriate candidates for a job. In modern organizations, recruitment activity increasingly takes place through websites and Applicant Tracking Systems, yet many recruitment tasks still depend heavily on language understanding. Recruiters must understand job requirements, interpret applicant resumes, evaluate the skills and experience being sought, compare candidates, reach a decision, and communicate clearly with candidates.

Because resumes, job descriptions, candidate feedback, and chatbot conversations are predominantly text-based, the role of Artificial Intelligence and Natural Language Processing in recruitment has grown substantially. Skills, education, work experience, training, certifications, and job requirements can be extracted from such documents using NLP techniques. Machine Learning and scoring-based techniques support resume classification, resume rank ordering, application shortlisting, and job-candidate matching. Semantic similarity techniques compare candidate profiles and job descriptions on the basis of meaning rather than exact keyword overlap. Large Language Models can generate job descriptions, summarize resumes, create feedback, explain skills, and support chatbot interactions during recruitment.

Recruitment trends indicate that a growing number of companies are adopting AI recruitment systems; LinkedIn reported that the share of recruiting firms using generative AI rose from 27% to 37% within one year [3]. If not properly controlled, however, AI-driven recruitment systems can generate unfair, ambiguous, or indefensible results [4], [7]. AI in recruitment should therefore act as a means of decision support that enhances efficiency, transparency, and candidate guidance, while still enabling employers and administrators to track recruitment outcomes.

# 2.4 Existing Systems / Frameworks / Designs

The literature review of existing recruitment systems falls into several categories: resume screening systems, semantic matching systems, job description generation systems, AI feedback systems, user profiling systems, job-candidate matching systems, chatbot-assisted recruitment systems, and AI governance studies. All of these categories relate to the research problem under consideration, but they typically apply to only a specific segment of the recruitment process, and are therefore not individually a complete answer to the whole recruitment journey.

The earliest recruitment automation tools were primarily concerned with screening resumes. Shahbaz [1] discussed automation of the recruitment process and demonstrated how activities can be made more efficient by reducing human effort. Yadav et al. [8] studied the use of NLP and ATS algorithms for evaluating resumes. These studies are relevant because they directly address resume screening; however, they do not extend to other vital recruitment activities such as forming job descriptions, presenting feedback to candidates, recognizing skill shortages, coordinating interviews, or supporting candidates more broadly.

A number of studies use machine learning and NLP for resume shortlisting and classification. Naik and Fal Dessai [9] proposed a technique for resume shortlisting and classification using machine learning. Kabir [10] introduced IntelCV, a smart approach for selecting skilled resumes. Kinge et al. [11] proposed employing Machine Learning and NLP methods for resume screening. These works demonstrate how ML and NLP can simplify the recruiter's task, but many of these systems offer candidates little insight into why they were, or were not, selected — they enhance automation without necessarily improving transparency.

More recent studies address resume screening using deep learning and transformer models. Gan et al. [12] presented a framework for resume screening using LLM agents. Deshmukh and Raut [13] applied S-BERT to improve resume screening and demonstrated the advantage of transformer-based models in understanding semantics. Mehboob et al. [14] evaluated automatic CV shortlisting systems using machine learning methods, and Mhatre et al. [15] used a convolutional neural network to rank and screen CVs. These studies demonstrate the possibilities of semantic ranking and deep learning, yet resumes arrive in many different formats, and the broader recruitment challenge extends well beyond semantic ranking alone.

Job description generation is another crucial research area. Qin et al. [1] proposed a capability-aware neural network for automatic job description generation. This work is highly relevant since job descriptions are the criteria used for candidate matching and ranking, yet many job description generation techniques are not linked to resume screening, skill-gap analysis, or candidate evaluation — generating job descriptions should not be a standalone process but part of a broader recruitment pipeline.

Research on generative AI shows that Large Language Models can be useful for analyzing resumes and generating feedback. Pranav and Hari [16] implemented a prototype AI system that uses LLMs for screening resumes. Patil et al. [17] introduced a GenAI-based approach to building an Applicant Tracking System in which skill-fitment analysis is central. Saxena et al. [18] studied the scope of LLMs for resume analysis and AI feedback loops. Venkatesh et al. [19] investigated automated resume analysis and skill suggestion using NLP. Lohith et al. [20] proposed an approach for resume analysis, skill enhancement, and job automation using NLP. These studies show the viability of resume analysis, recommendation, and candidate support using AI, but many lack a full role-oriented platform for candidates, employers, and administrators together.

Candidate feedback is a crucial element of transparent recruiting. Abhishek et al. [21] concentrated on AI-assisted resume recommendation during screening. Senghore et al. [22] proposed a person-job fit model using BERT for talent acquisition. Buddana et al. [23] studied text generation using LSTM and RNN architectures. Gan et al. [12] also studied LLM agents applied to recruitment tasks. These studies provide a useful background for building explanation and feedback systems with AI, yet many recruitment systems still do not give unsuccessful candidates feedback in a consistent way — in this research, feedback is treated as an integral component of the recruitment process rather than an add-on.

User profiling is another key element of intelligent recruitment. Sahoo [24] researched candidate profiling in the context of recruitment and selection. Sroison and Chan [25] discussed NLP techniques for resume parsing. Jiang et al. [26] proposed feature-fusion methods for person-job matching. This body of work reveals that structured candidate information can enhance matching accuracy, but most systems rely only on static resume data rather than a dynamic candidate profile. In this research, candidate profile, skills, resumes, CV information, and application history are all used for matching, skill-gap identification, feedback, and chatbot guidance.

Automatic job-candidate matching and notification form another important aspect of recruitment automation. Jiang et al. [26] highlighted the importance of feature fusion in person-job matching, yet many matching systems remain reactive, assessing candidates only once an application has been submitted. A recruitment system should ideally also support proactive matching, suggesting suitable jobs to candidates and notifying them of relevant recruitment activity; notification support is therefore included in the proposed system.

Chatbot use in recruitment has also been investigated. Monica et al. [27] investigated job seekers' attitudes toward AI chatbots in recruitment. Nikumbe et al. [6] developed a chatbot-supported AI portal for employment. Such research demonstrates the potential of chatbots to enhance communication and recruitment effectiveness, but chatbot assistance in these studies is typically restricted to simple questions or a single recruitment phase. In this research, chatbot support focuses on candidate profile improvement, explaining skill gaps, supporting job applications, interpreting feedback, and providing knowledge base assistance across roles.

There has also been a recent push in the literature toward auditing, transparency, and human control in AI-assisted recruitment. Wen et al. [4] introduced the FAIRE benchmark and demonstrated that LLM-based resume evaluations can be racially and gender biased. Aka et al. [28] found that AI-powered recruitment has the potential to enhance recruitment outcomes while also shaping the candidate selection process. Wilson et al. [7] demonstrated that AI recommendations can influence human decision-making in resume screening. These studies matter because they demonstrate that recruitment automation cannot depend exclusively on AI output — AI-driven recruitment solutions should feature explainable scoring, administrative oversight, audit trails, and the option for human intervention.

# 2.5 Technological Analysis

The technological analysis covers the algorithms, design methods, system architecture, and workflows of IRAS. This section is segmented into algorithmic analysis, design analysis, and workflow analysis, reflecting the intelligent recruitment capability delivered through AI-assisted and automated functions.

The analysis is narrowed to the role of technologies such as Natural Language Processing, Large Language Models, structured scoring algorithms, database management, API-based communication, CV PDF generation, notifications, and chatbot support within the system. This section is crucial since the research is software-engineering-oriented, and the technology chosen must be technically justifiable in relation to the proposed recruitment solution.

## 2.5.1 Algorithmic Analysis

There are several algorithmic approaches relevant to IRAS. The first is text extraction and pre-processing. The system must extract readable text from resumes and job descriptions, which may be in PDF, DOCX, or plain text format. The text must be cleaned so that symbols, unnecessary spacing, formatting artefacts, and irrelevant information are removed; accurate pre-processing has a direct impact on parsing, skill extraction, candidate ranking, and matching accuracy downstream.

The second is resume parsing and information extraction. Resume parsing retrieves crucial information such as name, contact details, education, work experience, certifications, technical skills, projects, and job-related skills. Named Entity Recognition can assist by identifying significant entities within unstructured resume text, though parsing may not work reliably for non-standard resume formats or missing information; candidates should therefore be able to review and edit the details extracted from their resume.

The third is skill extraction and normalization. Technical skills may be expressed in a variety of ways across resumes and job postings — for instance, "C# backend development" and "ASP.NET Core Web API" are related but not identical terms, while "JS" and "JavaScript" refer to the same concept. Extracted skills are mapped to a standardized skill taxonomy, which makes matching more accurate and reduces mismatches caused by inconsistent naming conventions.

The fourth is semantic similarity analysis. Traditional keyword matching looks for exact word overlap between a resume and a job description, whereas semantic similarity is based on meaning. Techniques such as TF-IDF, cosine similarity, word embeddings, sentence embeddings, BERT, and S-BERT can be used for this purpose. S-BERT is particularly useful since it can embed a resume and a job description as sentences and perform a semantic comparison between them [13]. In the proposed system, this gives an advantage over simple keyword matching, since related IT skills and IT roles can be expressed using a range of related terms.

The fifth is candidate ranking and scoring. Candidate suitability should not be evaluated using a single criterion; skill match, experience match, education match, qualification match, and semantic similarity can all inform ranking. A weighted scoring method is used to calculate the final match score, giving the ranking process more structure and explanation than a single opaque figure.

The sixth is skill-gap identification. The system compares a candidate's skills against those required for a selected job; gaps are identified where required skills are missing or under-developed, and these gaps are used to generate learning or improvement recommendations. This is significant because it is a development feature, not merely an elimination feature.

The seventh is LLM-based text generation. Employer-provided information such as job title, responsibilities, required skills, and experience level can be used to generate structured job descriptions through an LLM. LLMs can also facilitate feedback generation by explaining why a candidate might be a good or a poor fit for a position and what improvements are suggested; however, LLM output must be constrained to avoid irrelevant, biased, or fabricated content, and human review remains important for fairness and transparency [4], [7].

The eighth is CV generation. No machine learning model is required for the CV builder; it instead uses structured candidate profile data — education, experience, certifications, and skills — to produce a professional PDF document, allowing candidate information to be presented in a consistent, readable format.

The ninth is chatbot response generation. The chatbot is structured to accept only questions relevant to the system or the job search, helping candidates design their profile, understand their skill gaps, receive application guidance, and interpret feedback. The chatbot draws on a recruitment knowledge base and structured prompts to generate relevant responses; it should not function as a general-purpose chatbot, and candidate information should only be used in a privacy-conscious manner.

The tenth is evaluation measurement. Precision, recall, F1-score, and ranking comparison against human judgement can be used to evaluate candidate ranking. Extraction accuracy can be used to evaluate resume parsing. Skill-gap identification can be validated by comparing system-detected gaps against manually identified gaps. Relevance, completeness, clarity, and fairness are appropriate measures for evaluating AI-generated job descriptions and feedback, while relevance, accuracy, usefulness, and user satisfaction are appropriate measures of chatbot effectiveness. The system must be evaluated both as an AI-based recruitment system and as a viable software system, and all of these measures matter.

## 2.5.2 Design Analysis

IRAS is designed to serve three primary user roles — Candidate, Employer, and Admin — since responsibilities and system requirements differ across each group. Candidates need profile management, resume upload, job search, application tracking, skill-gap feedback, CV generation, notifications, and chatbot assistance. Employers need company profile management, job posting, AI job description generation, application review, candidate ranking, candidate comparison, interview scheduling, feedback management, and reports. Administrators must manage users, monitor job posts, manage skill taxonomy, manage the knowledge base, generate reports, review the audit log, monitor system status, and configure the system.

The system design is layered. The presentation layer offers the web-based user interface to candidates, employers, and administrators. The API layer processes incoming requests, validates user credentials, accesses other services, and exchanges data between frontend and backend. The application layer contains the essential business logic — recruitment processes, evaluation, matching, response generation, communications, resume handling, and chatbot operation. The domain layer represents the meaningful entities of the system: users, profiles, jobs, skills, interviews, responses, resumes, alerts, and audit logs. The infrastructure layer covers database operations, file management, and integration with the external AI service.

This layered approach is suitable because it is highly flexible and updatable. AI-related components can be revised as improved models, prompts, or algorithms become available — for example, the resume ranking algorithm, skill-gap explanation, feedback generation, and chatbot responses can be enhanced without redesigning the whole system, and the knowledge base and skill taxonomy can be improved incrementally for greater accuracy and relevance.

Security and confidentiality also play an important role in the system design, which includes authentication, role-based access control, secure file handling, and auditing, essential for protecting sensitive information. Candidates, resumes, recruitment decisions, applications, and job-offer details are all kept secure. Candidates only access their own profile, feedback, notifications, applications, and CV. Employers only access their own company profile, job posts, applications, interview information, and candidate evaluations. Platform-wide management and monitoring features are reserved for administrators.

In design terms, the proposed system is more powerful than any individual AI application because it combines AI decision support with workflow structure and system usability. Candidate ranking, skill-gap analysis, feedback generation, CV generation, interview scheduling, notifications, and chatbot support are not treated as individual tools; instead, they are incorporated into a single recruitment platform that helps employers make decisions while keeping candidates informed and the process transparent.

## 2.5.3 Workflow Analysis

The system is organized as a series of interconnected workflows supporting recruitment from job creation through to candidate feedback.

The first workflow is employer job creation. The employer logs in, enters minimal details about the vacancy, and uses the AI-powered job-description generator to produce a structured job description; the employer then edits and approves the content before publishing it. AI-generated job descriptions are never published without employer validation, since human intervention at this step is essential.

The second workflow is candidate profile and resume handling. A candidate registers, creates a profile, and uploads a resume with information on education, work experience, certifications, and skills. The system attempts to extract crucial information automatically from the resume file, but automated extraction is not always fully accurate given the diversity of resume formats, so the candidate can review and edit the extracted information.

The third workflow is application screening and candidate ranking. When candidates apply for a job, they are matched against job requirements using their profile, resume, skills, experience, and qualifications, producing a suitability or match score that allows the employer to view applicants ranked and compared more effectively.

The fourth workflow is skill-gap analysis. The system compares a candidate's current skills against those required for a particular position; gaps are identified where required skills are missing or under-developed, and candidates can view these gaps along with improvement tips and skill-development recommendations — a development feature, not simply an elimination feature.

The fifth workflow is automatic job-candidate matching. The system identifies candidates whose profiles and skills match a posted job's requirements and recommends or notifies suitable candidates accordingly, reducing the reliance on a purely passive recruitment platform where job seekers must search for every opportunity themselves.

The sixth workflow is CV generation. Candidates can use existing profile data — education, work experience, skills, and certifications — to create a professional CV in PDF format, helping them prepare a consistent, structured document from the data already held in the system.

The seventh workflow is interview scheduling. Once candidates have been reviewed and ranked, the employer can schedule an interview for shortlisted candidates; the system stores interview information and communicates scheduling details, improving coordination between employer and candidate.

The eighth workflow is the feedback process. When a candidate is turned down or asked to improve, the system provides feedback explaining why the candidate was unsuccessful and which skills should be developed, increasing transparency and helping candidates strengthen future applications.

The ninth workflow is notification handling. The system notifies users about job recommendations, application status changes, interview scheduling, feedback availability, and other recruitment information, improving communication between candidates and employers throughout the process.

The tenth workflow is chatbot support. Candidates can ask recruitment-related questions, learn about their skill gaps, receive tips on improving their profile, understand feedback, and get support with the application process through the chatbot, which is restricted to recruitment-relevant, knowledge-base-supported content to preserve relevance and accuracy.

# 2.6 Reflection

AI has been found to offer substantial benefit across the recruitment domain. Previous studies report positive results in resume screening, semantic matching, job description generation, skill matching, chatbot support, candidate profiling, and AI-assisted feedback. However, the existing literature is overwhelmingly focused on individual modules rather than a full recruitment ecosystem. This confirms the research gap identified in this study: recruitment automation needs to be delivered through an integrated system that includes job description creation, application shortlisting, candidate ranking, skill-gap analysis, candidate feedback, user profiling, automated job-candidate matching, CV creation, interview scheduling, notifications, knowledge base support, and chatbot assistance.

The review also demonstrates that recruitment automation should not only enhance employer efficiency; it should assist candidates by making the process more transparent and by guiding them toward improvement. Many AI systems can rank or filter candidates, but far fewer rank, explain, identify missing skills, and offer genuinely helpful feedback — an important gap given its direct effect on candidates' career prospects.

The literature further highlights the need for responsible use of AI in recruitment. LLM-based resume evaluation can carry racial and gender bias, as revealed by the FAIRE benchmark [4], and AI-recommendation studies show that biased AI recommendations can influence recruiter decisions [7]. As a result, the proposed system is treated as an open decision-support system rather than a fully automatic decision-maker: it provides structured scoring, explanations, feedback, and monitoring, but the final recruitment decision remains at the employer's discretion.

In conclusion, IRAS fills the identified void by integrating automation for employers, visibility for candidates, and oversight for administrators. For employers, this spans job description preparation, publishing job posts, evaluating applications, ranking candidates, comparing applicants, scheduling interviews, collecting feedback, and preparing reports. For candidates, it spans profile management, resume upload, job matching, skill-gap identification, CV composition, feedback, and chatbot notifications. For administrators, it spans user management, job tracking, skill taxonomy management, knowledge base maintenance, report management, and system supervision. All of the literature reviewed in this chapter dates from 2016 through mid-2026, with the substantial majority published within the last five years, ensuring the review reflects the current state of AI-assisted recruitment research.
