#C CHAPTER 04 - SYSTEM REQUIREMENT SPECIFICATION

# 4.1 Chapter Overview

This chapter translates the research objectives and methodology set out in Chapters 1 to 3 into a concrete System Requirement Specification (SRS) for the Intelligent Recruitment Automation System. It identifies the system's stakeholders and their concerns, describes how the requirements were operationalized from research objectives and data-gathering activity into concrete system behaviour, and presents the system and model analysis — use case diagram and specifications, class diagram, activity diagram, and sequence diagrams — that formed the design basis for the implementation described in Chapter 5. The chapter closes with the proposed system architecture and the consolidated functional and non-functional requirements.

# 4.2 Stakeholder Analysis

Three primary stakeholder roles interact with IRAS directly, and a fourth, indirect stakeholder — the external AI service — supplies the intelligence behind several of the system's automated features.

The **Candidate** is a job seeker who registers on the platform, builds a profile, uploads and manages a resume, searches and applies for jobs, receives proactive job matches, views their skill gap against a target role, works through a personalized skill-improvement plan, submits evidence of skill development, generates a CV, receives feedback and notifications, and uses the recruitment chatbot. The candidate's primary concern is fairness and transparency: understanding why a match score or rejection occurred and what concrete action would improve their standing.

The **Employer** is a hiring organization's representative who registers a company profile, drafts job postings with AI assistance, publishes vacancies (subject to admin moderation), reviews a ranked and explainable list of applicants, compares candidates, attaches a skill assessment to a role where relevant, schedules interviews, and issues feedback. The employer's primary concern is efficiency without losing trust in the ranking: a shortlist that is fast to produce but still auditable.

The **Administrator** is a platform operator responsible for user management, job-post moderation, skill-taxonomy curation, knowledge-base maintenance, audit-log review, AI-service health monitoring, and reporting. The administrator's primary concern is governance: ensuring that AI-influenced decisions across the platform remain within policy and can be reviewed after the fact.

The **AI Service** is a non-human, indirect stakeholder in the sense that its behaviour (resume parsing accuracy, semantic-similarity quality, fit-classifier confidence, and generated-text quality) directly shapes what every human stakeholder experiences, even though no human interacts with it directly; it is reached only through the core platform's backend.

These four stakeholders do not carry equal weight in every design decision. Where a candidate's transparency need and an employer's efficiency need pull in different directions — for instance, showing the full score breakdown takes marginally longer to render than a bare pass/fail badge — this specification consistently resolves in favour of the candidate's transparency concern, since Section 1.2 identified opaque, feedback-free rejection as the general problem this research set out to address in the first place; an efficient but unexplainable shortlist would solve the employer's problem while leaving the candidate's exactly where Chapter 1 found it.

TCAP:Table 4.1|Stakeholder Concerns and System Response.
| Stakeholder | Primary Concern | How IRAS Addresses It |
| Candidate | "Why was I rejected, and what should I improve?" | Explainable, weighted match score; AI-explained skill gap; personalized improvement plan with evidence review |
| Employer | "How do I shortlist quickly without missing a good fit?" | Multi-signal ranked applicant list broken down by skill, experience, education, and assessment score |
| Administrator | "Is the AI behaving, and can I prove it?" | Job moderation queue, audit log of AI-influenced actions, AI-service health monitoring, reporting |

The requirements presented in this chapter were not defined in the abstract; each was operationalized from a specific research objective (Section 1.7) and a specific fact-gathering activity (Section 3.4), and then reviewed for validity before being carried into design.

## 4.4.2 Class Diagram and Enhanced Entity-Relationship (EER) Diagram

FIG:class_diagram_full.png|Figure 4.2|Complete Class Diagram of the IRAS Domain Model. The diagram shows all thirty-six persistent entities in IRAS.Domain/Entities, organized into eight module swimlanes — Identity, Candidate Profile and CV, Skills and Development, Employer and Jobs, Applications and Interviews, Assessments, Engagement, and Admin and Governance — each box listing its primary key, foreign keys, and scalar attributes exactly as declared in source. Connector lines show every structural foreign-key relationship with its cardinality (1, 0..1, or *); single-field administrative "actor" references such as CreatedBy, VerifiedBy, ChangedBy, ScheduledBy, ApprovedBy, and UpdatedBy are annotated inline as (FK→User) on the attribute itself rather than drawn as separate connectors, to keep the diagram legible at this scale.

At this level of detail, three structural patterns repeat across modules and are worth naming once rather than per-entity. First, **profile-owned collections** (CandidateProfile → Education/WorkExperience/Certification/CandidateLanguage/CandidateProject/Resume/CvDocument) are all simple one-to-many children with no independent lifecycle — none of them is ever queried or matched against without going through the owning CandidateProfile first. Second, **many-to-many-with-attributes join entities** (CandidateSkill, JobRequiredSkill, CandidateTargetSkill) each carry a composite primary key of the two foreign keys they join plus additional scoring or status attributes (Proficiency/YearsExp, Importance/Weight, Status/AddedAt respectively) — they are not pure link tables, which is why each appears as its own box rather than as a bare connector. Third, **AI-review-gated entities** (SkillPlanEvidence, and to a lesser extent Feedback) each carry both a system-recommended value and a human-decision value side by side (AiConfidenceScore/AiRationale/AutoReviewed alongside VerifiedBy/VerifiedAt/VerifierNotes), which is the data-model expression of the confidence-gated review pattern described algorithmically in Section 5.1.1.

The class diagram above uses UML notation; Figure 4.3 presents the same underlying schema in Enhanced Entity-Relationship (EER) notation, which is the more conventional view for a database-design audience and additionally makes explicit two constructs UML class notation does not distinguish: which entities are structurally weak, and which relationship is a specialization hierarchy rather than a plain association.

FIG:eer_diagram_full.png|Figure 4.3|Enhanced Entity-Relationship (EER) Diagram of the IRAS Database. The diagram re-renders the same thirty-six entities in crow's-foot EER notation: every primary key is underlined, foreign keys are marked inline, and cardinality is shown with crow's-foot glyphs (a single bar for "exactly one," a circle-and-bar for "zero or one," and a circle-and-crow's-foot for "zero or many") rather than UML's plain-text multiplicities. CandidateSkill, JobRequiredSkill, and CandidateTargetSkill are drawn as double-bordered weak entities, since each is identified entirely by the composite of its two foreign keys rather than a surrogate key of its own. A purple ISA triangle marks User's specialization into CandidateProfile and EmployerProfile, labelled {disjoint, partial} — disjoint because a single user account is a candidate or an employer, never both, and partial because an Administrator account is neither subtype.

## 4.4.3 Activity Diagram

FIGWIDE:activity_diagram_full.png|Figure 4.4|Complete Activity Diagram of IRAS — Candidate, AI Service, Employer, and Administrator. The diagram traces the whole-system flow across four swimlanes, from registration through AI-assisted job description generation, admin moderation (with a reject-and-resubmit loop routed back to the employer), proactive batch matching, reactive application scoring with an assessment-required gate, a skill-gap-detected gate leading to AI-explained improvement plans and confidence-gated evidence review, employer shortlisting and a hiring-decision gate, AI-drafted candidate feedback, and the ongoing admin governance cycle, before looping back to the candidate's job search. The lane order (Candidate next to AI Service, rather than at opposite ends) is a deliberate layout choice: it is what keeps the many Candidate-AI transitions short, adjacent-lane hops instead of long diagonals cutting through the Employer and Administrator lanes' own content.

## 4.4.5 State Chart / Deployment Considerations

A full state chart is not required for every entity in the system, but two entities carry enough lifecycle complexity to warrant one: **Application**, which moves through Applied, Screened, Shortlisted, Interview, and then either Hired, Rejected, or Withdrawn; and **SkillPlanEvidence**, which moves through Draft, Pending, and then Approved, Rejected, or RevisionRequired, with RevisionRequired looping back to Draft for a resubmission. Both state machines are enforced at the application-service layer rather than left to the client, so that no combination of client requests can move either entity into an invalid state (for example, an application cannot be marked Hired without first passing through Interview).

FIG:state_chart_diagram.png|Figure 4.8|State Chart Diagrams — Application and SkillPlanEvidence. The upper diagram shows ApplicationStatus as a linear spine (Applied → Screened → Shortlisted → Interview → Hired), with a shared collector line making explicit that Withdrawn and Rejected are each reachable from any of the four non-terminal states while Hired is reachable only from Interview. The lower diagram shows EvidenceVerificationStatus as Draft → Pending branching three ways under AI/admin review (Approved, Rejected, or RevisionRequired), with two distinct loop-back transitions to Draft — one for a candidate withdrawing before a decision is reached, and one for a resubmission after a revision request — each routed on a separate channel so neither crosses the forward spine.

Both diagrams make the same underlying point in different notation to Figure 4.4's activity diagram: an activity diagram shows the *flow of work* across actors, while a state chart shows the *legal state space* of one entity in isolation — here, that every terminal state (the double-ringed circles) is reached only through the transitions drawn, which is what "enforced at the application-service layer" in the paragraph above actually means in implementation terms.

At the deployment level, the system is deployed as two independently deployable units — the ASP.NET Core Web API (containerized with Docker and hosted on Render.com) and the Python AI microservice (hosted separately and reached only over HTTP by the API) — against a managed SQL Server database, with Supabase providing object/file storage only. This separation allows the AI service to be redeployed, retrained, or temporarily taken offline (in which case the API degrades to skill-match-only scoring, as described in Table 4.3) without requiring a redeploy of the core platform.

# 4.5 Proposed System Architecture

FIG:architecture_diagram.png|Figure 4.9|IRAS Layered System Architecture. The diagram shows the five layers of the system from top to bottom — Presentation (the React/TypeScript candidate, employer, and admin portals), API (ASP.NET Core controllers, JWT authentication, and Swagger documentation), Application (the business services that implement matching, skill-gap analysis, assessments, chat, and CV generation), Domain (the persistent entities), and Infrastructure (the database, file storage, and the AI-service HTTP client) — together with the external Python AI service that the Application layer calls over HTTP for resume parsing, semantic ranking, and fit-classification.

The architecture follows a conventional layered (N-tier) design rather than a more elaborate pattern such as CQRS or microservices for the core platform, because the domain does not currently require independently scalable write/read paths, and a single well-tested API deployment is easier for a solo final-year project to operate and reason about than a distributed set of internal services. The one place the system does adopt a service boundary is around AI functionality specifically, where the cost/benefit is different: the AI workload (resume parsing, embeddings, and the trained classifier) has a different technology stack (Python, not .NET), different scaling characteristics (CPU/GPU-bound inference rather than request/response CRUD), and a materially different failure mode (a model or third-party LLM outage should degrade the platform, not take it down) — all reasons that justify carving it out as its own deployable service even while the rest of the system stays a layered monolith.

# 4.7 Chapter Summary

This chapter specified the system requirements for IRAS by first identifying its stakeholders and their concerns, then tracing each requirement back to a research objective and a fact-gathering activity to keep the specification grounded rather than speculative. The use case, class, activity, and sequence diagrams presented the system and model analysis that the implementation in Chapter 5 follows, the layered architecture diagram fixed the system's structural boundaries, and the consolidated functional and non-functional requirement tables provide the checklist against which the testing and evaluation in Chapter 6 is conducted.
