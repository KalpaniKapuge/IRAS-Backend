FIGURES
Figure 1.1: Rich Picture of the Proposed System. The diagram shows the Candidate, Employer/Company, and Admin user groups surrounding the central Intelligent Recruitment Automation System, together with the AI service layer, database and file storage, skill taxonomy, knowledge base, and the ranking/matching/skill-gap/feedback engine that the core system depends on. Arrows are labelled with the main information exchanged between each user group and the core platform (for example, "uploads resume / applies" and "posts jobs / reviews ranking"), showing that all interaction is mediated through the authenticated core system rather than occurring directly between user groups.
Figure 2.1: Conceptual Map of the Literature. The diagram places the proposed "AI-Supported Integrated Recruitment Automation System" at the centre and surrounds it with the eight literature categories reviewed in this chapter — resume screening and parsing, automatic job description generation, user profiling, AI governance and transparency, semantic job-candidate matching, recruitment chatbot support, skill-gap analysis and feedback, and automated notifications and scheduling — each annotated with its strength and its main gap relative to an integrated platform.
Figure 3.1: Design Science Research Methodology Execution Workflow. The diagram shows the six sequential stages followed in this research — Problem Identification, Relevance Justification, Comparative Analysis, Objectives Finalization, Design and Development, and Evaluation and Communication — with each box annotated underneath by the concrete evidence base used at that stage, for example recruitment pain points at the identification stage and prototype and data handling at the design and development stage.
Figure 3.2: Project Timeline. The Gantt-style chart shows six project phases spanning the academic year — Inception and Planning, Data Acquisition and Preparation, Core Model Development, System Development and Integration, Evaluation and Testing, and Final Documentation and Closure — plotted month by month from November through September, with each phase broken down into its constituent tasks such as the job-description generator, the CV screening and ranking engine, the chatbot assistant, and the skill-gap analysis and feedback module.
Figure 4.1: Complete Use Case Diagram of IRAS with «include» / «extend» Relationships. The diagram shows all twenty-six use cases across the three human actors — Candidate (11), Employer (7), and Administrator (8) — against the Intelligent Recruitment Automation System boundary, together with eight explicit UML dependency relationships: four «include» links (a mandatory sub-behaviour always performed as part of the base use case, e.g. Post/Publish Job always includes Generate Job Description) and four «extend» links (an optional behaviour triggered only under a stated condition, e.g. Attempt Skill Assessment extends Search & Apply for Jobs only when the job's RequireAssessment flag is set). The shared Authenticate precondition and the AI Service's supporting role are recorded as a boxed note rather than drawn as connectors, since a line from one corner to a dozen scattered use cases is exactly the kind of visual collision the rest of the diagram was deliberately laid out to avoid.
Figure 4.2: Complete Class Diagram of the IRAS Domain Model. The diagram shows all thirty-six persistent entities in IRAS.Domain/Entities, organized into eight module swimlanes — Identity, Candidate Profile and CV, Skills and Development, Employer and Jobs, Applications and Interviews, Assessments, Engagement, and Admin and Governance — each box listing its primary key, foreign keys, and scalar attributes exactly as declared in source. Connector lines show every structural foreign-key relationship with its cardinality (1, 0..1, or *); single-field administrative "actor" references such as CreatedBy, VerifiedBy, ChangedBy, ScheduledBy, ApprovedBy, and UpdatedBy are annotated inline as (FK→User) on the attribute itself rather than drawn as separate connectors, to keep the diagram legible at this scale.
Figure 4.3: Enhanced Entity-Relationship (EER) Diagram of the IRAS Database. The diagram re-renders the same thirty-six entities in crow's-foot EER notation: every primary key is underlined, foreign keys are marked inline, and cardinality is shown with crow's-foot glyphs (a single bar for "exactly one," a circle-and-bar for "zero or one," and a circle-and-crow's-foot for "zero or many") rather than UML's plain-text multiplicities. CandidateSkill, JobRequiredSkill, and CandidateTargetSkill are drawn as double-bordered weak entities, since each is identified entirely by the composite of its two foreign keys rather than a surrogate key of its own. A purple ISA triangle marks User's specialization into CandidateProfile and EmployerProfile, labelled {disjoint, partial} — disjoint because a single user account is a candidate or an employer, never both, and partial because an Administrator account is neither subtype.
Figure 4.4: Complete Activity Diagram of IRAS — Candidate, AI Service, Employer, and Administrator. The diagram traces the whole-system flow across four swimlanes, from registration through AI-assisted job description generation, admin moderation (with a reject-and-resubmit loop routed back to the employer), proactive batch matching, reactive application scoring with an assessment-required gate, a skill-gap-detected gate leading to AI-explained improvement plans and confidence-gated evidence review, employer shortlisting and a hiring-decision gate, AI-drafted candidate feedback, and the ongoing admin governance cycle, before looping back to the candidate's job search. The lane order (Candidate next to AI Service, rather than at opposite ends) is a deliberate layout choice: it is what keeps the many Candidate-AI transitions short, adjacent-lane hops instead of long diagonals cutting through the Employer and Administrator lanes' own content.
Figure 4.5: Sequence Diagram — Job Application and Scoring. The diagram traces a candidate's POST /applications request through ApplicationsController and ApplicationService to ScoringService, which calls the external AI service's /rank endpoint for semantic similarity and a fit score, computes the taxonomy-based skill match locally, and persists the combined application score and any skill-gap rows before returning a 201 Created response to the candidate.
Figure 4.6: Sequence Diagram — Resume Upload and Parsing. The diagram shows a candidate's resume file being saved to file storage by CvService before being sent to the AI service's /parse-resume endpoint, which returns the parsed text and detected skills; CvService then persists the Resume and CandidateSkill rows and returns a preview the candidate can review and correct before it is treated as authoritative.
Figure 4.7: Sequence Diagram — Skill Evidence Submission and Review. The diagram shows a candidate's evidence submission moving to Pending status, being assessed by the AI Evidence Reviewer for a recommendation and confidence score, and — only when that confidence is low — being escalated to an Administrator who applies the final Approved/Rejected decision, which is then written back to the evidence's verification status and surfaced to the candidate.
Figure 4.8: State Chart Diagrams — Application and SkillPlanEvidence. The upper diagram shows ApplicationStatus as a linear spine (Applied → Screened → Shortlisted → Interview → Hired), with a shared collector line making explicit that Withdrawn and Rejected are each reachable from any of the four non-terminal states while Hired is reachable only from Interview. The lower diagram shows EvidenceVerificationStatus as Draft → Pending branching three ways under AI/admin review (Approved, Rejected, or RevisionRequired), with two distinct loop-back transitions to Draft — one for a candidate withdrawing before a decision is reached, and one for a resubmission after a revision request — each routed on a separate channel so neither crosses the forward spine.
Figure 4.9: IRAS Layered System Architecture. The diagram shows the five layers of the system from top to bottom — Presentation (the React/TypeScript candidate, employer, and admin portals), API (ASP.NET Core controllers, JWT authentication, and Swagger documentation), Application (the business services that implement matching, skill-gap analysis, assessments, chat, and CV generation), Domain (the persistent entities), and Infrastructure (the database, file storage, and the AI-service HTTP client) — together with the external Python AI service that the Application layer calls over HTTP for resume parsing, semantic ranking, and fit-classification.
Figure 5.1: Weighted Scoring Pipeline. The block diagram shows the four independent signals — skill match, semantic similarity, ML fit score, and assessment score — feeding into a single configurable weighted sum, which is then persisted as the Application's or JobMatch's headline score and compared against the auto-match threshold; the asterisked note records that the ML fit-score and assessment-score weights default to zero so existing deployments are unaffected until an operator opts in, even though both signals are still computed and stored.

Figure 6.1: Full-Feature vs Text-Only Model Accuracy. The grouped bar chart plots the exact test accuracy of all four algorithms under the full-feature configuration against the same algorithms restricted to text-only input (Tables 6.5/6.7), showing a gap of roughly 25 to 44 percentage points for every algorithm and confirming that the engineered skill-overlap and semantic-similarity features, not the free text alone, are what drive the model's accuracy.
Figure 6.2: Confusion Matrices — Full-Feature Models. The four heatmaps show the exact cell-by-cell confusion matrix behind each full-feature model's Table 6.5 row (values reproduced in Table 6.8). Random Forest is visibly the weakest of the four off the diagonal — 7 of 75 Potential Fit records misclassified as No Fit and 6 as Good Fit — while Logistic Regression and SVM misclassify only 1-2 records each and XGBoost only 1, all concentrated on the No Fit/Potential Fit boundary rather than the more consequential No Fit/Good Fit confusion, which every model avoids almost entirely.
Figure 6.3: ROC and Precision-Recall Curves — XGBoost (Full-Feature). The one-vs-rest ROC curves reach an AUC of 0.998 for No Fit, 0.997 for Potential Fit, and 1.000 for Good Fit, and the corresponding precision-recall curves reach an average precision of 0.997, 0.988, and 1.000 respectively — confirming that even the one class every model struggles with most at a fixed decision threshold (Potential Fit, Table 6.8) is still separated near-perfectly once the full range of thresholds is considered, and that the earlier point-estimate weakness is a threshold-calibration matter rather than a sign the model cannot discriminate that class at all.
Figure 6.4: Learning Curves — Text-Only Feature Set (Accuracy and Log Loss vs. Training Set Size). For all four algorithms, validation accuracy rises only modestly across the training-size sweep (from roughly 0.42-0.47 up to 0.50-0.63) and stays well below the 0.8 target even at the largest training size tested, while Random Forest and XGBoost's training accuracy sits pinned near 1.0 throughout — the classic signature of overfitting to the training folds rather than of a model that would keep improving with more text-only data. Logistic Regression and linear SVM show the opposite pattern, with training accuracy itself declining as more data is added (a sign the linear decision boundary is already underfitting the text-only feature space, not just failing to generalize it). Read together with Figure 6.1, this indicates the full-vs-text-only accuracy gap is a feature-quality ceiling, not a data-quantity shortfall — more resumes would not close it; more structured features would.
Figure 6.5: Cross-Validation Accuracy vs. Untouched Holdout Test Accuracy, by Model and Feature Set. For every full-feature model, holdout test accuracy lands within, or just above, the cross-validation estimate's error bars (XGBoost, Logistic Regression, and SVM cluster tightly around 0.98-0.99 on both axes; Random Forest's holdout accuracy of 0.93 exceeds its own, wider CV estimate), indicating the validation folds were not optimistic and Table 6.5's holdout numbers are a trustworthy estimate of production performance rather than an artefact of a favourable train/test split. The text-only models show the same non-optimistic pattern but with visibly larger error bars — the weaker feature set is also the less stable one, reinforcing Figure 6.4's overfitting finding from a different angle.

The diagram covers UC-13 and UC-14 together with UC-20: the employer's AI-drafted job description is only ever saved as Draft, and only an administrator's explicit approval moves it to Published, at which point the flow hands off to the proactive matching sequence in Figure C.3.

The diagram shows JobMatchingService retrieving every opted-in, resume-parsed candidate for a newly published job, sending them to the AI service in a single batched /rank call rather than one call per candidate, and creating a JobMatch plus notification only for the candidates whose combined score clears the configured threshold.
Figure C.4: Sequence Diagram — Skill Gap Explanation & Improvement Plan Generation. The diagram covers UC-07: comparing a candidate's skills against a job's requirements, generating a plain-language AI explanation of the gap, and — on the candidate's request — generating a personalized, sequenced improvement plan.
Figure C.5: Sequence Diagram — Skill Assessment: Generation, Attempt & AI Grading. The diagram covers UC-09 and UC-16: a job's assessment questions are generated once (by the AI service) and reused for every subsequent candidate, an attempt is scored automatically for multiple-choice questions and by the AI grader for free-text answers, and the combined result is persisted against the attempt.
Figure C.6: Sequence Diagram — Interview Scheduling & Candidate Feedback. The diagram covers UC-17 and UC-18: an employer schedules an interview against a shortlisted application, then, following a hiring decision, an AI-drafted feedback message is generated, reviewed and approved by the employer, and only then delivered to the candidate.
Figure C.7: Sequence Diagram — Role-Aware Chatbot Interaction. The diagram covers UC-10: the role-specific context builder assembles only that authenticated user's own data before the LLM call, and the reply is passed through ChatScopeGate before delivery, so an out-of-scope question is deflected rather than answered.
